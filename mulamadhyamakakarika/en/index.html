About this edition

Summary

This volume contains the complete root verses of Nāgārjuna’s Mūlamadhyamakakārikā in twenty-seven chapters. Each verse is given in Tibetan (Derge D3824) with Wylie, in Sanskrit in Devanagari and IAST with its source marked, and in English, Portuguese and Spanish. The translation follows the Tibetan line by line and was checked against the Sanskrit.

The Tibetan text opens with the translator’s homage to Mañjuśrī and two dedication verses, and closes with a homage to Gautama and the colophon of the Tibetan translation, which names the translators Jñānagarbha and Cokro Lui Gyaltsen and the later revisers Hasumati, Patsab Nyima Drak and Kanaka. The chapters examine, in order: conditions, going and coming, the faculties, the aggregates, the elements, desire and the desirous one, arising and abiding and disintegration, agent and action, what abides prior, fire and fuel, the prior and later limits, what is made by self and by another, formations, contact, intrinsic nature, bondage and liberation, action, self and phenomena, time, the assemblage, coming to be and disintegration, the Tathāgata, error, the noble truths, nirvāṇa, the twelve links of becoming, and views.

The Sanskrit has 448 numbered verses and the Derge text has 449 four-line units including dedication and final homage. Verse 3.7 has no Tibetan counterpart, and verses 11.7 and 11.8 are ordered differently in the two languages. Readings of the Derge e-text that look doubtful are flagged with a dagger and listed in the editorial notes.

Chapter 1

Examination of Conditions

pratyayaparīkṣā

Translator’s homage (Tibetan only)

Homage to Mañjuśrī, the youthful one!

Dedication verses

The one who taught that dependent origination

is without cessation, without arising,

without annihilation, without permanence,

without coming, without going,

neither different in meaning nor one in meaning,

the complete pacification of elaborations, peace:

to that perfect Buddha, the best of speakers,

to that supreme one, I prostrate!

1.1†

Not from self, not from another,

not from both, nor without a cause,

for any things, anywhere,

does arising ever exist.

1.2

There are four conditions:

cause, supporting object, immediately preceding,

and likewise the dominant.

A fifth condition does not exist.

1.3

The intrinsic nature of things

does not exist in conditions and the rest.

If own being does not exist,

other being does not exist.

1.4†

Action does not possess conditions.

Action without conditions does not exist.

Conditions without action are not conditions.

Or do conditions that possess action exist?

1.5

Because it arises in dependence on these,

these are therefore called conditions.

As long as it has not arisen,

why are these not nonconditions?

1.6

For a thing that is nonexistent or existent,

a condition is not admissible.

If nonexistent, of what would it be the condition?

If existent, what would a condition do?

1.7

When a phenomenon is not established

as existent, nonexistent, or both,

how could a cause be called a producer?

If so, this is not reasonable.

1.8

The supporting object of this existent phenomenon

is taught to be precisely nonexistent.

If a phenomenon has no supporting object,

how could a supporting object exist?

1.9

If phenomena have not arisen,

cessation is not tenable.

Therefore the immediately preceding is not reasonable.

If it has ceased, what would be the condition?

1.10

Because things without intrinsic nature

have no existence,

the statement “When this exists, this arises”

is not tenable.

1.11

In the conditions, separate or combined,

that result does not exist at all.

What does not exist in the conditions,

how could it arise from the conditions?

1.12

If, though nonexistent,

it arose from those conditions,

why would it not also arise

from nonconditions?

1.13

If the result has the nature of conditions,

the conditions do not have a nature of their own.

A result from what has no being of its own,

how could it have the nature of conditions?

1.14†

Therefore a result with the nature of conditions

or with the nature of nonconditions

does not exist. Since the result does not exist,

how could there be conditions or nonconditions?

The first chapter, called Examination of Conditions.

Chapter 2

Examination of Going and Coming

gatāgataparīkṣā

2.1†

First, there is no going on what has been traversed.

Nor is there going on what has not been traversed.

Apart from the traversed and the not traversed,

what is being traversed cannot be known.

2.2

Where there is movement, there is going.

That movement is in what is being traversed,

not in the traversed and not in the not traversed.

Therefore going is in what is being traversed.

2.3

That going is in what is being traversed,

how could that be tenable,

when, without going,

what is being traversed is not tenable?

2.4

For whoever holds that going is in what is being traversed,

it would follow that what is being traversed

is without going,

because going is in what is being traversed.

2.5

If going is in what is being traversed,

two goings would follow:

the one by which it is being traversed,

and the going that is in it.

2.6

If two goings follow,

there would also be two goers,

because without a goer

going is not tenable.

2.7

If there is no goer,

going is not tenable.

If there is no going,

how could a goer exist at all?

2.8

First, a goer does not go.

A nongoer does not go.

Other than a goer and a nongoer,

what third one goes?

2.9

When without going

a goer is not tenable,

to say that a goer goes,

how could that be tenable at all?

2.10

For whoever holds the position that a goer

goes, it would follow

that there is a goer without going,

because they assert that a goer goes.

2.11

If a goer goes,

two goings would follow:

the one by which the goer is manifest as a goer,

and the one that is gone once there is a goer.

2.12

Going does not begin in the traversed.

Going does not begin in the not traversed either.

If it does not begin in what is being traversed,

where does going begin?

2.13

Before going begins,

where going would begin,

there is nothing being traversed and nothing traversed.

How could there be going in the not traversed?

2.14

If the beginning of going, in every way,

does not appear at all,

what traversed, what being traversed,

what not traversed can be conceived?

2.15

First, a goer does not stay.

A nongoer does not stay.

Other than a goer and a nongoer,

what third one stays?

2.16

When without going

a goer is not tenable,

to say that a goer stays,

how could that be tenable at all?

2.17†

There is no stopping from what is being traversed,

nor from the traversed or the not traversed.

Going, starting,

and stopping are the same as going.

2.18

That going and the goer

are the very same is not admissible to say.

That going and the goer

are different is not admissible to say either.

2.19

If whatever is going

were itself the goer,

it would follow that agent and action

are one.

2.20

If going and goer

are conceived as different,

there would be going without a goer

and a goer without going.

2.21

If these two, as one thing

or as different things,

are not established,

how could the two be established?

2.22

The going by which a goer is manifest as a goer,

that going the goer does not go,

because the goer does not exist before going.

Who goes, and where?

2.23

The going by which a goer is manifest as a goer,

a going other than that the goer does not go,

because in a single goer

two goings are not tenable.

2.24

One who is a goer

does not go a going of the three kinds.

One who is not a goer

does not go a going of the three kinds either.

2.25

One who both is and is not a goer

does not go a going of the three kinds either.

Therefore going, goer,

and what is to be traversed do not exist.

The second chapter, called Examination of Going and Coming.

Chapter 3

Examination of the Faculties

cakṣurādīndriyaparīkṣā

3.1

Seeing, hearing, smelling,

tasting, touching, and mind

are the six faculties. Their

fields of experience are the visible and so on.

3.2

That seeing does not see

its own self at all.

What does not see itself,

how could it see others?

3.3†

To fully establish seeing,

the example of fire is not adequate.

By the traversed, the not traversed, and what is being traversed,

that, together with seeing, has been answered.

3.4

When it is not seeing anything at all,

it is not something that sees.

To say that seeing sees,

how could that be reasonable?

3.5

Seeing itself does not see.

Nonseeing itself does not see.

By seeing itself, the seer too

should be understood as explained.

3.6

Without setting seeing aside, there is no seer,

nor when seeing has been set aside.

If there is no seer, how could there be

the visible and the seeing?

3.7†

Just as the birth of a child is said to depend on mother and father,

so the arising of consciousness is said to depend on eye and form.

3.8

Because the visible and seeing do not exist,

the four, consciousness and so on,

do not exist. How, then, could appropriation and so on

come to exist?

3.9

By seeing, hearing, smelling,

tasting, touching, and mind,

the hearer, the heard, and so on

should be understood as explained.

The third chapter, called Examination of the Faculties.

Chapter 4

Examination of the Aggregates

skandhaparīkṣā

4.1

Apart from the cause of form,

form is not observed.

Apart from what is called form,

the cause of form does not appear either.

4.2

If form existed apart from the cause of form,

it would follow that form

is without a cause. But nothing whatsoever

exists anywhere without a cause.

4.3

If, apart from form,

there were a cause of form,

it would be a cause without a result.

There is no cause without a result.

4.4

If form exists, a cause of form

is not tenable at all.

If form does not exist, a cause of form

is not tenable at all.

4.5

Forms without a cause

are not tenable, not admissible at all.

Therefore one should not conceive

any conceptions about form.

4.6

To say that the result is similar to the cause

is not tenable.

To say that the result is dissimilar to the cause

is not tenable either.

4.7

Feeling, perception, formations,

mind, and all things as well,

in every respect

follow the same procedure as form.

4.8

When a dispute has been made by means of emptiness,

whoever speaks a reply,

for them everything is unanswered.

It becomes the same as what is to be proven.

4.9

When an explanation has been made by means of emptiness,

whoever speaks a criticism,

for them everything is uncriticized.

It becomes the same as what is to be proven.

The fourth chapter, called Examination of the Aggregates.

Chapter 5

Examination of the Elements

dhātuparīkṣā

5.1

Before the characteristic of space,

there is not the slightest space.

If it existed before the characteristic,

it would follow that it is without characteristic.

5.2

A thing without characteristic

does not exist anywhere at all.

If a thing without characteristic does not exist,

to what would a characteristic apply?

5.3

A characteristic does not apply to what has no characteristic,

nor to what has a characteristic.

Nor does it apply to anything other

than what has and what has no characteristic.

5.4

If the characteristic does not apply,

the characterized is not tenable.

If the characterized is not tenable,

the characteristic does not exist either.

5.5

Therefore the characterized does not exist,

and the characteristic does not exist at all.

Apart from the characterized and the characteristic,

a thing does not exist either.

5.6

If a thing does not exist,

of what would there be a nonthing?

Who, being unlike thing and nonthing,

knows thing and nonthing?

5.7

Therefore space is not a thing,

not a nonthing, not the characterized,

not a characteristic. The other five elements

are also the same as space.

5.8

Those of small intelligence who see things

as existent and as nonexistent

do not see the peace,

the complete pacification of what is to be seen.

The fifth chapter, called Examination of the Elements.

Chapter 6

Examination of Desire and the Desirous One

rāgaraktaparīkṣā

6.1

If before desire

there were a desirous one without desire,

desire would exist in dependence on that one.

Given a desirous one, desire would exist.

6.2†

If there is no desirous one either,

how could desire exist?

For the desirous one too, whether desire

exists or not, the procedure is the same.

6.3

Desire and the desirous one

cannot reasonably arise together,

for then desire and the desirous one

would not depend on each other.

6.4

In oneness there is no togetherness.

A thing is not together with itself.

But if there is difference,

how could there be togetherness?

6.5

If what is one were together,

that would occur even without a companion.

If what is different were together,

that would also occur even without a companion.

6.6

If what is different is together,

are desire and the desirous one

established as different,

so that the two are together?

6.7

If desire and the desirous one

are established as different,

why do you imagine

that they are together?

6.8

Because they are not established as different,

you therefore assert togetherness.

To fully establish togetherness,

do you again assert difference?

6.9

Because the state of difference is not established,

the state of togetherness is not established.

Given what state of difference

do you assert the state of togetherness?

6.10

Thus desire and the desirous one

are not established as together or as not together.

Like desire, all phenomena

are not established as together or as not together.

The sixth chapter, called Examination of Desire and the Desirous One.

Chapter 7

Examination of Arising, Abiding, and Disintegration

saṃskṛtaparīkṣā

7.1

If arising is conditioned,

it would possess the three characteristics.

If arising is unconditioned,

how is it a characteristic of the conditioned?

7.2

The three, arising and so on, taken separately,

are not able to do the work

of characterizing the conditioned. How could they be admissible

gathered in one thing at one time?

7.3

If arising, abiding, and disintegration

had another characteristic of the conditioned,

there would be an infinite regress.

If not, they are not conditioned.

7.4

The arising of arising produces

only the root arising.

The root arising in turn

produces the arising of arising.

7.5

If your arising of arising

produces the root arising,

how could it, not produced by your root,

produce that?

7.6

If what is produced by your root

produces the root,

how could that root, not produced by it,

produce it?

7.7

If that which is unarisen

were able to produce that,

then you may assert as you wish

that what for you is arising produces it.

7.8

Just as a lamp illuminates

itself and others,

so arising too produces both

its own being and that of others, if that is what is said.

7.9

In the lamp and wherever

it is present, there is no darkness.

What does the lamp illuminate?

It illuminates by clearing away darkness.

7.10

When a lamp that is arising

does not meet darkness,

how does a lamp that is arising

clear away darkness?

7.11

If a lamp, even without meeting it,

clears away darkness,

the darkness that abides in the whole world

would be cleared by the one that abides here.

7.12

If a lamp illuminates

its own being and that of others,

darkness too, without doubt,

would obscure its own being and that of others.

7.13

Since this arising is unarisen,

how could it produce itself?

If it produces once arisen,

being arisen, what is there to produce?

7.14

The arisen, the unarisen, and what is arising

are not produced in any way.

This has been explained by the traversed, the not traversed,

and what is being traversed.

7.15

When, though arising exists,

this which is arising does not come forth,

how can it be said that, in dependence on arising,

it is arising?

7.16

Whatever arises dependently

is peace by its very essence.

Therefore what is arising

and arising itself are peace.

7.17

If some unarisen thing

existed somewhere,

it could arise. If that thing

does not exist, what would arise?

7.18

If that arising

produces what is arising,

what arising, in turn,

produces that arising?

7.19

If another arising produces it,

there is an infinite regress.

If it arises without an arising,

everything would arise in that way.

7.20

First, for the existent and for the nonexistent,

arising is not reasonable.

Nor for what is both existent and nonexistent.

This was already shown above.

7.21

For a thing that is ceasing,

arising is not tenable.

What is not ceasing

is not tenable as a thing.

7.22

A thing that has abided does not abide.

A thing that has not abided does not abide.

What is abiding does not abide either.

What unarisen thing could abide?

7.23

For a thing that is ceasing,

abiding is not tenable.

What is not ceasing

is not tenable as a thing.

7.24

If all things at all times

have the nature of aging and death,

what things are there that abide

without aging and death?

7.25

Abiding does not reasonably abide

through another abiding or through itself,

just as arising is not produced

by itself or by another.

7.26

The ceased does not cease.

The unceased does not cease either.

Likewise what is ceasing does not.

What unarisen thing could cease?

7.27

First, for a thing that abides,

cessation is not tenable.

For a thing that does not abide,

cessation is not tenable either.

7.28

A state does not itself

cease through that same state.

A state does not cease

through another state either.

7.29

When the arising

of all phenomena is not tenable,

then the cessation

of all phenomena is not tenable.

7.30

First, for an existent thing,

cessation is not tenable.

In oneness, a thing

and a nonthing are not tenable.

7.31

For a nonexistent thing too,

cessation is not tenable,

just as a second head

cannot be cut off.

7.32

Cessation does not exist through itself.

Cessation is not through another,

just as arising is not produced

by itself or by another.

7.33

Because arising, abiding, and disintegration

are not established, the conditioned does not exist.

Since the conditioned is not established at all,

how could the unconditioned be established?

7.34

Like a dream, like an illusion,

like a city of gandharvas,

so arising, so abiding,

and so disintegration have been taught.

The seventh chapter, called Examination of Arising, Abiding, and Disintegration.

Chapter 8

Examination of Agent and Action

karmakārakaparīkṣā

8.1

One who really is an agent

does not perform what really is an action.

One who is not really an agent

does not perform what is not really an action either.

8.2

For what really is, there is no activity,

and the action would be without an agent.

For what really is, there is no activity,

and the agent would be without an action.

8.3

If one who is not really an agent

performed what is not really an action,

the action would have no cause,

and the agent too would have no cause.

8.4

If there is no cause, result

and cause are not tenable.

If those do not exist, activity,

agent, and instrument are not reasonable.

8.5

If activity and the rest are not reasonable,

Dharma and non-Dharma do not exist.

If Dharma and non-Dharma do not exist,

the result that arises from them does not exist.

8.6

If there is no result, the path to liberation

and to the higher realms is not tenable.

And all activities

would follow to be pointless.

8.7

One who both is and is not an agent

does not perform what both is and is not.

Being and not being are mutually contradictory.

How could they exist in one?

8.8

By one who is an agent,

an action that is not is not performed.

By one who is not, one that is is not performed either.

Here too those same faults would follow.

8.9†

One who is an agent

does not perform an action that is not,

or one that both is and is not,

because of the reasons shown above.

8.10†

One who is not an agent

does not perform an action that is,

or one that both is and is not,

because of the reasons shown above.

8.11

One who both is and is not an agent

does not perform an action that is

or that is not. This too should be understood

by the reasons shown above.

8.12

The agent depends on the action,

and the action arises in dependence

on that very agent. Apart from this,

we do not see a cause for their establishment.

8.13

Appropriation should be understood in the same way,

because action and agent have been eliminated.

By agent and action

the remaining things should be understood.

The eighth chapter, called Examination of Agent and Action.

Chapter 9

Examination of What Abides Prior

pūrvaparīkṣā

9.1

Seeing, hearing, and so on,

and feeling and so on, which are governed,

the one to whom they belong

exists prior to them, some say.

9.2

If that thing did not exist,

how could seeing and so on occur?

Therefore, prior to them,

that abiding thing exists.

9.3

To seeing, hearing, and so on,

and to feeling and so on,

whatever thing abides prior,

by what is it designated?

9.4

If, even without seeing and so on,

that one abides,

then they too, even without that one,

would exist without doubt.

9.5

Someone is made manifest by something.

Something is made manifest by someone.

Without something, how could there be someone?

Without someone, how could there be something?

9.6

Prior to all of seeing and so on,

no one exists.

By one or another among seeing and so on,

at one or another time, that one is made manifest.

9.7

If that one does not exist prior

to all of seeing and so on,

how could that one exist prior

to each of seeing and so on?

9.8

If the seer is the very hearer,

and the feeler too is that very one,

that one would exist prior to each.

That is not reasonable in this way.

9.9

If the seer is one,

the hearer another, and the feeler another,

when there is a seer there would be a hearer,

and the selves would be many.

9.10

Seeing, hearing, and so on,

and feeling and so on as well,

in the elements from which they arise

that one does not exist either.

9.11

Seeing, hearing, and so on,

and feeling and so on as well,

if the one to whom they belong does not exist,

they do not exist either.

9.12

For that which does not exist prior to,

together with, or after seeing and so on,

the conceptions “it exists” and “it does not exist”

come to a stop.

The ninth chapter, called Examination of What Abides Prior.

Chapter 10

Examination of Fire and Fuel

agnīndhanaparīkṣā

10.1

If the fuel is the fire,

agent and action would be one.

If fire is other than fuel,

it would arise even without fuel.

10.2

It would burn forever.

It would not arise from a cause of burning.

Starting it would be pointless.

If so, it would also be without action.

10.3

Because it does not depend on another,

it would not arise from a cause of burning.

If it burns forever,

starting it would be pointless.

10.4

If, regarding this, one thinks

that what is being burned is the fuel,

when it is only that much,

by what is that fuel burned?

10.5

Being other, it does not meet. Not meeting,

it does not burn. Not burning,

it does not die out. Not dying out,

it remains, endowed with its own mark.

10.6

Just as a woman meets a man

and a man meets a woman,

it would be fit to meet with fuel.

10.7

If fire and fuel

If fire depends on fuel,

If a thing is established in dependence,

what it depends on is established,

There is no fuel that depends on fire.

There is no fuel that does not depend on fire either.

Those who teach that the self and things

are identical or different,

I do not think that they

are skilled in the meaning of the teaching.

Chapter 17

Examination of Action

karmaphalaparīkṣā

17.1

Restraining oneself well,

benefiting others, and the mind of love,

that is Dharma. It is the seed

of results here and elsewhere.

17.2

The Supreme Sage said that actions

are intention and the intended.

The distinctions of those actions

are proclaimed to be of many kinds.

17.3

Of these, the action called intention

is held to be mental.

What is called the intended

is that of body and speech.

17.4

Speech, movement, and what are called

the nonrevealing forms of nonabstention,

and the other nonrevealing forms

of abstention, held to be likewise,

17.5

merit arising from use,

nonmerit of the same kind,

and intention: these seven phenomena

are held to be manifest as action.

17.6

If it abides until the time of ripening,

that action would be permanent.

If it has ceased, then, having ceased,

how could it produce a result?

17.7

The continuum of the sprout and so on

comes forth from the seed.

From that comes the fruit. Without the seed,

that would not come forth either.

17.8

Since from the seed comes the continuum,

and from the continuum the fruit arises,

the seed precedes the fruit.

Therefore there is no annihilation and no permanence.

17.9

Whatever is the continuum of mind

comes forth from mind.

From that comes the result. Without mind,

that would not come forth either.

17.10

Since from mind comes the continuum,

and from the continuum the result arises,

action precedes the result.

Therefore there is no annihilation and no permanence.

17.11

The ten paths of white action

are the means of accomplishing Dharma.

The results of Dharma, here and elsewhere,

are the five qualities of sense pleasure.

17.12

If that conception were so,

there would be many great faults.

Therefore that conception

is not tenable here.

17.13

The conception that was taught by the Buddhas,

the pratyekabuddhas, and the śrāvakas,

and that is tenable here,

that I will fully state.

17.14†

Like a promissory note is the unperishing,

and action is like a debt.

In terms of realms it is of four kinds.

And by nature it is unspecified.

17.15

It is not abandoned by abandonment.

It is abandoned only by meditation.

Therefore by the unperishing

the result of actions is produced.

17.16

If it were abandoned by abandonment,

or destroyed by the transference of action,

then faults such as the destruction of action

would follow.

17.17

For all actions of the same realm,

similar and dissimilar,

at the moment of rebirth linking

only a single one arises.

17.18

In this life, for each and every action

of the two kinds,

it arises separately,

and it abides even after ripening.

17.19

It ceases upon transition to the result

or upon death.

Its division should be known

as uncontaminated and contaminated.

17.20†

Emptiness, yet no annihilation.

Saṃsāra, yet no permanence.

The unperishing of actions

is the Dharma taught by the Buddha.

17.21

Why does action not arise?

Because it is without intrinsic nature.

And because it is unarisen,

it does not perish.

17.22

If action had intrinsic nature,

it would without doubt be permanent.

Action would be unmade,

because for the permanent there is no making.

17.23

If action were unmade,

there would be fear of meeting what one has not done.

And not abiding in pure conduct

would follow there as a fault.

17.24

All conventions too

would without doubt be contradicted.

The distinction between those who do merit

and those who do negativities would not be tenable either.

17.25

What has already ripened

would ripen again and again,

if intrinsic nature existed,

because the action would remain.

17.26

This action has the nature of afflictions,

and those afflictions are not real.

If the afflictions are not real,

how could action be real?

17.27

Action and afflictions

are taught as the conditions of bodies.

If action and afflictions

are empty, what can be said of bodies?

17.28†

The being obscured by ignorance

and possessing craving is the experiencer.

That one is not other than the agent,

nor is that one the very same.

17.29

Because this action

does not arise from conditions,

and does not arise from nonconditions,

therefore the agent does not exist either.

17.30

If there is no action and no agent,

how could there be a result born of action?

If there is no result,

how could there be an experiencer?

17.31

Just as the Teacher emanates an emanation

through perfect miraculous power,

and that emanation in turn

emanates yet another emanation,

17.32

so the agent and the action done by it

are like the form of an emanation.

It is like an emanation

emanating another emanation.

17.33

Afflictions, actions, bodies,

agents, and results

are like a city of gandharvas,

like a mirage and a dream.

If it arises from the assemblage

If it arises from the assemblage

never exists.

20.13

20.14

An unborn result with a born cause,

Chapter 22

Examination of the Tathāgata

that one would now depend,

What has not been appropriated

the Tathāgata who does not exist

as the same or as other,

how could that one be designated?

22.11

One should not say “empty.”

One should not say “nonempty” either,

nor “both,” nor “neither.”

These are stated for the purpose of designation.

22.12

The four, permanent, impermanent, and so on,

how could they exist in this peace?

The four, having a limit, having no limit, and so on,

how could they exist in this peace?

22.13

One who holds the dense grasping

that the Tathāgata exists

conceives the conception

that in nirvāṇa that one does not exist.

22.14

With regard to that which is empty by intrinsic nature,

the thought that the Buddha, after nirvāṇa,

exists or does not exist

is not tenable at all.

22.15

Those who elaborate the Buddha,

who is beyond elaboration and inexhaustible,

all of them, impaired by elaboration,

do not see the Tathāgata.

22.16

Whatever is the intrinsic nature of the Tathāgata

is the intrinsic nature of these wandering beings.

The Tathāgata has no intrinsic nature.

These wandering beings have no intrinsic nature.

Chapter 23

Examination of Error

viparyāsaparīkṣā

23.1

Desire, hatred, and delusion

are said to arise from conceptualization.

In dependence on the pleasant, the unpleasant,

and error they arise.

23.2

Those that arise in dependence on the pleasant,

the unpleasant, and error

do not exist from intrinsic nature.

Therefore the afflictions are not real.

23.3

The existence and nonexistence of the self

are not established in any way.

Without it, how could the existence and nonexistence

of the afflictions be established?

23.4

The one to whom these afflictions belong

is not established either.

Without anyone,

there are no afflictions of anyone at all.

23.5

As in the view of one’s own body, the afflictions

do not exist in the afflicted in the five ways.

As in the view of one’s own body, the afflicted

does not exist in the afflictions in the five ways.

23.6

If the pleasant, the unpleasant, and error

do not exist from intrinsic nature,

on what pleasant, unpleasant, and error

do the afflictions depend?

23.7

Forms, sounds, tastes, tangibles,

smells, and phenomena, the six kinds,

are conceived to be the basis

of desire, hatred, and delusion.

23.8

Forms, sounds, tastes, tangibles,

smells, and phenomena are merely

like a city of gandharvas,

like a mirage and a dream.

23.9

In those that are like an illusory person

and like a reflection,

the pleasant and the unpleasant,

how could they come to occur?

23.10

The unpleasant, in dependence on which

the pleasant is designated,

does not exist without relying on the pleasant.

Therefore the pleasant is not tenable.

23.11

The pleasant, in dependence on which

the unpleasant is designated,

does not exist without relying on the unpleasant.

Therefore the unpleasant is not tenable.

23.12

If the pleasant does not exist,

how could desire exist?

If the unpleasant does not exist,

how could hatred exist?

23.13

If to grasp the impermanent as permanent

is in that way an error,

since in the empty there is nothing impermanent,

how could grasping be an error?

23.14

If to grasp the impermanent as permanent

is in that way an error,

to grasp the empty as impermanent,

how is that not also an error?

23.15

That by which one grasps, the grasping,

the grasper, and what is grasped

are all completely pacified.

Therefore grasping does not exist.

23.16

Whether wrong or right,

if there is no grasping,

who would have error,

and who would have no error?

23.17

For one who is already in error,

errors are not possible.

For one who is not in error,

errors are not possible.

23.18

For one who is falling into error,

errors are not possible.

For whom errors are possible,

examine that for yourself.

23.19

If errors have not arisen,

how could they exist?

If errors are without arising,

how could there be one who has error?

23.20

A thing does not arise from itself.

It does not arise from another at all.

If not from itself and another either,

how could there be one who has error?

23.21

If self, purity,

permanence, and happiness exist,

then self, purity, permanence,

and happiness are not errors.

23.22

If self, purity,

permanence, and happiness do not exist,

then no self, impurity, impermanence,

and suffering do not exist.

23.23

Thus, through the cessation of error,

ignorance ceases.

When ignorance has ceased,

formations and the rest cease.

23.24

If someone’s afflictions

existed by intrinsic nature,

how could they be abandoned?

Who could abandon the existent?

23.25

If someone’s afflictions

did not exist by intrinsic nature,

how could they be abandoned?

Who could abandon the nonexistent?

The twenty-third chapter, called Examination of Error.

Chapter 24

Examination of the Noble Truths

āryasatyaparīkṣā

24.1

If all these are empty,

there is no arising and no disintegration.

The four noble truths

would follow not to exist for you.

24.2†

Because the four noble truths do not exist,

complete knowledge, abandonment,

meditation, and realization

are not tenable.

24.3

Because those do not exist,

the four fruits do not exist either.

Without the fruits there are none who abide in the fruits,

nor are there those who have entered.

24.4

If those eight kinds of persons

do not exist, there is no Saṅgha.

Because the noble truths do not exist,

the sacred Dharma does not exist either.

24.5†

If Dharma and Saṅgha do not exist,

how could a Buddha exist?

If you speak of emptiness in this way,

you harm the Three Jewels,

24.6

and the existence of results,

non-Dharma and Dharma itself,

and the conventions of the world,

all of these too you harm.

24.7

To this it is explained: you have not understood

the purpose of emptiness, emptiness itself,

and the meaning of emptiness.

Therefore you are harmed in this way.

24.8

The Dharma taught by the Buddhas

relies entirely on two truths:

the relative truth of the world

and the truth of the ultimate meaning.

24.9

Those who do not understand

the distinction between these two truths

do not understand the profound reality

in the Buddha’s teaching.

24.10

Without relying on convention,

the ultimate meaning cannot be taught.

Without realizing the ultimate meaning,

nirvāṇa is not attained.

24.11

By viewing emptiness wrongly,

those of little prajñā are ruined,

like a snake wrongly grasped

or a vidyā mantra wrongly accomplished.

24.12

Therefore, knowing that the depths of this Dharma

are difficult for the weak to fathom,

the mind of the Sage turned completely away

from teaching the Dharma.

24.13

Since a consequence of fault

is not tenable for the empty,

your rejection of emptiness,

whatever it is, is not tenable for me.

24.14

For whom emptiness is admissible,

for them everything is admissible.

For whom emptiness is not admissible,

for them nothing is admissible.

24.15

You, turning your own faults

over onto me,

are like one who, while mounted on a horse,

has forgotten that very horse.

24.16

If you regard things

as existing from intrinsic nature,

then, that being so, you regard things

as without causes and conditions.

24.17

Result and cause itself,

agent, instrument, and activity,

arising and cessation,

and the result as well you harm.

24.18

Whatever is dependent origination,

that is explained to be emptiness.

That is dependent designation.

That itself is the path of the Middle Way.

24.19

Because a phenomenon that is not dependently arisen

does not exist at all,

therefore a phenomenon that is not empty

does not exist at all.

24.20

If all this is nonempty,

there is no arising and no disintegration.

The four noble truths

would follow not to exist for you.

24.21

If it is not dependently originated,

how could suffering exist?

The impermanent that is said to be suffering

does not exist in what has intrinsic nature.

24.22

If it exists from intrinsic nature,

what would originate?

Therefore, for one who harms emptiness,

origin does not exist.

24.23

If suffering exists by intrinsic nature,

cessation does not exist.

Because it would remain entirely by intrinsic nature,

you harm cessation.

24.24

If the path had intrinsic nature,

meditation would not be tenable.

If that path is to be meditated on,

your intrinsic nature does not exist.

24.25

When suffering, origin,

and cessation do not exist,

what cessation of suffering

do you assert the path will attain?

24.26

If, by intrinsic nature itself,

it is not completely known,

how will it come to be completely known?

Does intrinsic nature not remain?

24.27

Likewise, for you,

abandonment, realization,

meditation, and the four fruits as well

are not admissible, just like complete knowledge.

24.28

For one who fully holds to intrinsic nature,

a fruit that by intrinsic nature itself

is not attained,

how could it be possible to attain it?

24.29

Without the fruits there are none who abide in the fruits,

nor are there those who have entered.

If those eight kinds of persons

do not exist, there is no Saṅgha.

24.30

Because the noble truths do not exist,

the sacred Dharma does not exist either.

If Dharma and Saṅgha do not exist,

how could a Buddha exist?

24.31

For you it would follow that a Buddha

does not even depend on bodhi.

For you it would follow that bodhi

does not even depend on a Buddha.

24.32

For you, one who by intrinsic nature

is not a Buddha,

though striving for bodhi in the conduct of bodhi,

will not attain bodhi.

24.33

No one will ever do

Dharma or non-Dharma.

What is there to do for the nonempty?

For intrinsic nature there is no doing.

24.34

Even without Dharma and non-Dharma,

the result would exist for you.

A result arisen from the causes of Dharma and non-Dharma

does not exist for you.

24.35

If a result arisen from the causes of Dharma and non-Dharma

exists for you,

a result arisen from Dharma and non-Dharma,

why is it not empty?

24.36

Whoever harms the emptiness

of dependent origination

harms all

the conventions of the world.

24.37

If one harms emptiness,

there would be nothing at all to do.

There would be doing that has not begun.

Even one who does not do would be an agent.

24.38

If intrinsic nature existed, wandering beings

would be unborn and unceased,

would remain immutable,

and would be devoid of varied states.

24.39†

If the empty does not exist,

attaining what is unattained,

the action that ends suffering,

and the abandonment of all afflictions do not exist either.

24.40

Whoever sees dependent origination

sees suffering,

origin, cessation,

and the path itself.

The twenty-fourth chapter, called Examination of the Noble Truths.

Chapter 25

Examination of Nirvāṇa

nirvāṇaparīkṣā

25.1

If all these are empty,

there is no arising and no disintegration.

From the abandonment or cessation of what

is passing into nirvāṇa asserted?

25.2

If all this is nonempty,

there is no arising and no disintegration.

From the abandonment or cessation of what

is passing into nirvāṇa asserted?

25.3

Not abandoned, not attained,

not annihilated, not permanent,

not ceased, not arisen:

this is said to be nirvāṇa.

25.4

First, nirvāṇa is not a thing.

It would follow that it has the characteristics of aging and death.

A thing without aging and death

does not exist.

25.5

If nirvāṇa were a thing,

nirvāṇa would be conditioned.

A thing that is not conditioned

does not exist anywhere at all.

25.6

If nirvāṇa were a thing,

how would that nirvāṇa be nondependent?

A thing that is not dependent

does not exist at all.

25.7

If nirvāṇa is not a thing,

how could a nonthing be admissible?

Where nirvāṇa is not a thing,

there a nonthing does not exist.

25.8†

If nirvāṇa is a nonthing,

how would that nirvāṇa be nondependent?

A nonthing that is not dependent

does not exist.

25.9

The state of coming and going,

which is dependent or caused,

when not dependent and not caused

is taught to be nirvāṇa.

25.10†

Coming to be and disintegration

are to be abandoned: this is the authoritative teaching of the Teacher.

Therefore it is reasonable that nirvāṇa

is neither a thing nor a nonthing.

25.11†

If nirvāṇa were

both a thing and a nonthing,

liberation would be thing and nonthing.

That is not reasonable.

25.12

If nirvāṇa were

both a thing and a nonthing,

nirvāṇa would not be nondependent,

because it would depend on those two.

25.13

How could nirvāṇa be

both a thing and a nonthing?

Nirvāṇa is unconditioned.

Thing and nonthing are conditioned.

25.14

How could there exist in nirvāṇa

both thing and nonthing?

Those two do not exist in one place,

like light and darkness.

25.15

Neither a thing nor a nonthing:

whoever teaches nirvāṇa so,

if nonthing and thing

were established, that would be established.

25.16

If nirvāṇa is

neither a thing nor a nonthing,

“neither a thing nor a nonthing,”

by whom is that made manifest?

25.17

After the Bhagavān has passed into nirvāṇa,

it is not evident that the Bhagavān exists. Likewise

“does not exist,” or “both,”

or “neither” is not evident either.

25.18

Even while the Bhagavān is abiding,

it is not evident that the Bhagavān exists. Likewise

“does not exist,” or “both,”

or “neither” is not evident either.

25.19

Saṃsāra, from nirvāṇa,

has not the slightest distinction.

Nirvāṇa, from saṃsāra,

has not the slightest distinction.

25.20

Whatever is the limit of nirvāṇa

is the limit of saṃsāra.

Between those two there is not the slightest difference,

not even the most subtle.

25.21

The views about what is beyond passing away, about limits and so on,

and about permanence and so on,

depend on nirvāṇa, the later limit,

and the prior limit.

25.22

Since all things are empty,

what has a limit? What has no limit?

What both has a limit and has no limit?

What neither has a limit nor has no limit?

25.23

What is the same? What is other?

What is permanent? What is impermanent?

What is both permanent and impermanent?

What is neither?

25.24

The complete pacification of all observation,

the complete pacification of elaboration, is peace.

The Buddha did not teach, anywhere,

any Dharma to anyone.

The twenty-fifth chapter, called Examination of Nirvāṇa.

Chapter 26

Examination of the Twelve Links of Becoming

dvādaśāṅgaparīkṣā

26.1

Obscured by ignorance, for the sake of rebirth

the three kinds of formations

are formed. By those actions

one goes to a destiny.

26.2

Consciousness, conditioned by formations,

enters into the destinies.

When consciousness has entered,

name and form develop.

26.3

When name and form have developed,

the six sense bases come to be.

In dependence on the six sense bases,

contact truly arises.

26.4†

On eye, form, and attention

it depends, and only so does it arise.

Thus in dependence on name and form

consciousness arises.

26.5

Eye, form, and consciousness,

the coming together of these three

is contact. From that contact

feeling arises.

26.6

Conditioned by feeling there is craving.

One craves for the sake of feeling.

When there is craving, the four kinds

of appropriation are appropriated.

26.7

When there is appropriation, the becoming

of the appropriator fully arises.

If there were no appropriation,

one would be liberated and there would be no becoming.

26.8

That becoming is the five aggregates.

From becoming comes birth.

Aging and death, sorrow,

lamentation, suffering,

26.9

mental unhappiness, and distress,

these come forth from birth.

Thus this entire

mass of suffering comes to be.

26.10

The root of saṃsāra is formations.

Therefore the wise do not form them.

Therefore the unwise are the agents.

The wise are not, because they see reality.

26.11

When ignorance has ceased,

formations too do not come to be.

The cessation of ignorance

comes from meditating on reality with knowledge.

26.12

Through the cessation of this and that,

this and that do not come forth.

This entire mass of suffering

thus truly ceases.

The twenty-sixth chapter, called Examination of the Twelve Links of Becoming.

Chapter 27

Examination of Views

dṛṣṭiparīkṣā

27.1†

“In the past time I existed” and “I did not exist,”

and that the world is permanent and so on:

whatever such views there are

depend on the prior limit.

27.2

“In another, future time I will exist”

and “I will not exist,” and that the world has a limit and so on:

whatever such views there are

depend on the later limit.

27.3

The statement “I existed in the past time”

is not tenable.

Whoever existed in former lives

is not this very one.

27.4

If you think that very one is the self,

the appropriation would be different.

Apart from appropriation,

what is your self?

27.5

When it is posited that there is no self

apart from appropriation,

if the appropriation itself is the self,

your self does not exist.

27.6

The appropriation itself is not the self.

It arises and disintegrates.

The appropriated,

how could it be the appropriator?

27.7

The self, as other than appropriation,

is not tenable at all.

If it were other, it should be apprehensible

without appropriation, but it is not apprehended.

27.8

Thus it is not other than appropriation,

nor is it the appropriation itself.

The self is not without appropriation,

nor is it ascertained as nonexistent.

27.9

The statement “I did not exist in the past time”

is not tenable either.

Whoever existed in former lives,

this one is not other than that.

27.10

If this one were other,

it would arise even without that one.

Likewise that one would remain,

and one would be born there without having died.

27.11

Annihilation, the perishing of actions,

and actions done by one

being experienced by another,

and so on, would follow.

27.12

It does not arise from not having existed,

for here a fault would follow.

The self would be something made,

or it would arise without a cause.

27.13

Thus “I existed,” “I did not exist,”

both, and neither:

whatever such views about the past there are,

they are not tenable.

27.14

“In another, future time I will exist”

and “I will not exist”:

whatever such views there are

are the same as with the past time.

27.15

If the god is the human,

then there would be permanence.

The god would be unborn,

because for the permanent there is no birth.

27.16

If the human is other than the god,

then there would be impermanence.

If god and human are other,

a continuum is not tenable.

27.17

If one part were god

and one part were human,

there would be both permanence and impermanence.

That too is not reasonable.

27.18

If permanence and impermanence,

both, were established,

then neither permanent nor impermanent,

you may assert as you wish, would be established.

27.19†

If someone came from somewhere

and also went somewhere,

then saṃsāra would be without beginning.

But that does not exist.

27.20

If nothing permanent exists,

what would be impermanent?

What would be both permanent and impermanent,

or what excludes those two?

27.21

If the world has a limit,

how could there be a next world?

If the world has no limit,

how could there be a next world?

27.22

Because this continuum of the aggregates

is like the light of a lamp,

therefore having a limit

and having no limit are not reasonable.

27.23

If the former ones disintegrated,

and in dependence on these aggregates

those aggregates did not arise,

then the world would have a limit.

27.24

If the former ones did not disintegrate,

and in dependence on these aggregates

those aggregates did not arise,

then the world would have no limit.

27.25

If one part had a limit

and one part had no limit,

the world would both have and not have a limit.

That too is not reasonable.

27.26

How could one part of the appropriator

completely disintegrate

while one part does not disintegrate?

In that way this is not reasonable.

27.27

How could one part of the appropriated

completely disintegrate

while one part does not disintegrate?

In that way this too is not reasonable.

27.28

If having a limit and having no limit,

both, were established,

then neither having nor not having a limit,

you may assert as you wish, would be established.

27.29

Or else, because all things

are empty, the views of permanence and so on,

which ones, where, for whom,

and from what would they arise?

27.30†

The one who, moved by compassion,

in order to abandon all views,

taught the sacred Dharma,

to that Gautama I prostrate!

The twenty-seventh chapter, called Examination of Views.

Colophon of the Tibetan Translation

This Root Verses on the Middle Way, called Prajñā, sets forth the Abhidharma of the Great Vehicle. It perfectly teaches the reality of the ultimate. It clarifies the way of the Perfection of Prajñā. It was composed by the Ācārya, the great being, the noble Nāgārjuna, who was endowed with unassailable knowledge and compassion. That one clarified the way of the unsurpassed vehicle of the Tathāgata, accomplished the ground of Perfect Joy, and went to the realm of Sukhāvatī. In the world realm called Light of Clarity, that one will become the Tathāgata called Light of the Source of Wisdom. It is complete.

By the decree of the sovereign lord, the great king, the glorious divine Tsenpo, the great Indian upādhyāya, the Mādhyamika Jñānagarbha, and the chief editor and translator, the bhikṣu Cokro Lui Gyaltsen, translated, edited, and finalized it. It has twenty-seven chapters and four hundred and forty-nine ślokas. It was made into one and a half bampo.

Later, in the center of the Kashmiri city of Anupama, in the Ratnagupta temple, the Kashmiri upādhyāya Hasumati and the Tibetan translator Patsab Nyima Drak, during the reign of the lord of men Āryadeva, revised it in accordance with the commentary Clear Words.

Later, in the Rasa Trulnang temple, the Indian upādhyāya Kanaka and that same translator made the final edit.
