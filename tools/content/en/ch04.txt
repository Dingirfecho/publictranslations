At that time, the Venerables Subhūti, Mahākātyāyana, Mahākāśyapa, and Mahāmaudgalyāyana, hearing from the Buddha this unprecedented Dharma and seeing the World-Honored One confer upon Śāriputra the prophecy of anuttarasamyaksambodhi, gave rise to a rare mind, rejoicing and leaping.
Immediately rising from their seats, they straightened their robes, bared their right shoulders, placed their right knees on the ground, single-mindedly joined their palms, bowed reverently, gazed up at the venerable countenance, and addressed the Buddha:
We stand at the head of the Saṅgha, old and decrepit. Considering that we had already attained nirvāṇa, we felt incapable of further responsibility and no longer advanced to seek anuttarasamyaksambodhi.
In the past, when the World-Honored One expounded the Dharma for a long time, we were in our seats, our bodies weary and sluggish, contemplating only emptiness, signlessness, and aimlessness; regarding the Bodhisattva dharmas—sporting in spiritual powers, purifying Buddha lands, and perfecting wandering beings—our minds felt no delight.
For what reason? The World-Honored One caused us to emerge from the three realms and attain the realization of nirvāṇa. Furthermore, now we are old and decrepit; regarding the Buddha's teaching and transforming Bodhisattvas in anuttarasamyaksambodhi, we did not give rise to a single thought of fond aspiration.
Now, before the Buddha, hearing the prophecy of anuttarasamyaksambodhi conferred upon a Śrāvaka, our hearts rejoice greatly, having attained what we never had before. We never thought that today we would suddenly hear this rare Dharma, deeply congratulating ourselves on gaining great wholesome benefit; immeasurable precious treasures, unsought, have arrived of themselves!
World-Honored One! We now wish to speak a parable to clarify this meaning.
Suppose there is a man who in his youth leaves his father and runs away. He dwells long in another country—for ten, twenty, or up to fifty years. As he grows older, he falls into destitution, running about in the four directions seeking food and clothing. Gradually wandering, he happens to head toward his native country.
His father had earlier sought his son without finding him, settling in a certain city. His household is exceedingly wealthy, his treasures boundless: gold, silver, lapis lazuli, coral, amber, and crystal beads, his various storehouses completely overflowing. He has many servants, ministers, assistants, officials, and citizens; elephants, horses, carriages, oxen, and sheep beyond count. His lending and trade extend throughout other countries, and merchant traders are numerous.
At that time, the poor son, wandering through villages, countries, and towns, arrives at the city where his father resides.
The father constantly thought of his son, separated from him for over fifty years, yet had never spoken of this matter to others. He merely reflected within himself, harboring regret and grief, thinking: "I am old and decrepit, possessing abundant wealth, gold, silver, and treasures, my storehouses overflowing; yet I have no son. Once I pass away, my property will scatter and be lost, having none to entrust it to." Therefore, he earnestly recalled his son, further thinking: "If I find my son and entrust my wealth to him, I will be joyful and carefree, having no further anxiety."
World-Honored One! At that time, the poor son, wandering as a hired laborer, happens to arrive at his father's house, standing by the gate.
From afar, he sees his father seated upon a lion seat, his feet resting on a jeweled footstool, reverently surrounded by brāhmaṇas, kṣatriyas, and householders. Strands of pearls and necklaces worth tens of millions adorn his body; officials, citizens, and servants holding white whisks stand attendant at his left and right. Over him is a precious canopy, hanging with flower banners; scented water is sprinkled on the ground, rare flowers are scattered, and treasures are arrayed, receiving and distributing goods—such various adornments as these, his majesty and virtue extraordinarily revered.
The poor son, seeing his father possessing such great power, is immediately terrified, regretting having come there. Secretly he thinks: "This is perhaps a king or the equal of a king, not a place where I can hire out my labor to obtain goods. It would be better to go to a poor village where labor is easy to find and clothing and food readily obtained. If I remain here long, I might be oppressed and forced to work." Having thought this, he runs away in haste.
Then the wealthy elder, seated upon the lion seat, recognizes his son at first sight. His heart rejoicing greatly, he thinks: "My wealth and storehouses now have someone to be entrusted to. I constantly thought of this son without a means to see him, and suddenly he has arrived by himself, perfectly matching my wish. Although I am old, I still cherish him."
He immediately dispatches an attendant to pursue and bring him back. The messenger runs swiftly and seizes him. The poor son, startled and alarmed, cries out in grievance: "I have done nothing against you, why am I seized?" The messenger grips him more tightly, dragging him back by force.
Then the poor son, thinking that though blameless he has been captured and will certainly be put to death, becomes still more terrified, faints, and falls to the ground.
The father, seeing this from afar, says to the messenger: "There is no need for this man; do not bring him by force. Sprinkle cold water on his face to revive him, and speak to him no further."
Why is this? The father knows his son's disposition is base and inferior, knowing that his own nobility and wealth were intimidating to the son. Ascertaining that it is his son, he uses expedient means, not telling others: "This is my son." The messenger says to him: "I now release you; go wherever you please." The poor son rejoices, having attained what he never had before, rises from the ground, and goes to a poor village to seek food and clothing.
At that time, the elder, wishing to attract his son, devises an expedient means. He secretly dispatches two men of emaciated appearance and no impressive demeanor: "You go there and speak gently to the poor son: 'There is a workplace here that will pay you double wages.' If the poor son agrees, bring him here to work. If he asks: 'What work is to be done?' you may say: 'You are hired to clear dung; we two will also work with you.'"
The two messengers immediately seek out the poor son, and having found him, explain the matter fully.
Then the poor son first takes his wage, and then joins them in clearing dung. His father, seeing his son, pities and wonders at him.
On another day, through a window, he sees his son's body from afar, emaciated and haggard, covered with dung, dust, and grime, filthy and impure. He immediately removes his necklaces, fine soft upper robes, and decorative ornaments, putting on coarse, ragged, and dirty garments, covering his body with dust, holding a dung basket in his right hand, appearing unassuming.
He addresses the workers: "Work diligently, do not slacken." By this expedient means, he is able to approach his son.
Afterward, he addresses him: "Hey, my friend! You must constantly work here, do not go elsewhere; I will increase your wages. Whatever you need—pots, utensils, rice, flour, salt, and vinegar—do not hesitate; I also have an old servant whom you can ask for whatever you need, so put your mind at ease. I am like a father to you. Have no further anxiety.
For what reason? I am old in years, while you are young and strong. When you work, you are free of deceit, sloth, anger, resentment, and grumbling; I have never seen you have these evils like the other workers. From now on, you are like my own son, flesh and blood."
Immediately, the elder gives him a name, calling him his child. At that time, although the poor son rejoiced at this encounter, he still regarded himself as a lowly hired laborer. For this reason, throughout twenty years he was kept constantly clearing dung.
After this time passed, they came to trust each other, and he entered and exited without difficulty, though his lodging remained in the original place.
World-Honored One! Then the elder fell ill, knowing that he would die before long. He addressed the poor son: "I now possess abundant gold, silver, and treasures, my storehouses overflowing; the quantities within them, what should be received and given, you know completely. My intention is like this; you must understand this intent.
For what reason? Now between me and you, there is no difference; apply your mind carefully, do not allow anything to be lost."
Then the poor son, receiving his instruction, took charge of all the goods, gold, silver, treasures, and storehouses, yet harbored no desire to take even a single meal's worth. His lodging remained in the original place, and he was still unable to abandon his inferior mindset.
After a short while, the father knew his son's mind had gradually broadened, accomplishing great resolve and despising his previous humble attitude.
Approaching death, he summoned his son and gathered his relatives, the king, ministers, kṣatriyas, and householders, all assembling together. He publicly announced:
"Gentlemen, you should know! This is my son, flesh and blood. In a certain city, he abandoned me and ran away, enduring hardship and wandering for over fifty years. His original name was such-and-such; my name was so-and-so. In the past, in my original city, I sought him with sorrow; suddenly, in this place, I encountered and found him. This is truly my son, and I am truly his father. Now all my property belongs to my son; all that was previously received and disbursed is known to this son."
World-Honored One! At that time, the poor son, hearing his father's words, rejoiced greatly, having attained what he never had before, thinking: "I originally had no mind to seek anything, yet now this jewel treasury has arrived spontaneously!"
World-Honored One! The wealthy elder is the Tathāgata, and we are all like Buddha-children; the Tathāgata constantly calls us his children.
World-Honored One! Because of the three sufferings, we underwent various torments within birth and death, deluded and ignorant, joyfully attached to small dharmas. Today, the World-Honored One causes us to contemplate, clearing away the dung of discursive play of all dharmas; within this, we diligently exerted vigor, attaining the single day's wage of nirvāṇa. Having attained this, our hearts rejoiced greatly, considering ourselves satisfied, and we claimed: "Because of diligent vigor within the Buddha-Dharma, what we have attained is vast!"
Yet the World-Honored One previously knew that our minds were attached to base desires and delighted in small dharmas, so he indulged us, not distinguishing for us: "You will have a share in the Tathāgata's treasury of knowledge and vision."
The World-Honored One, through the power of expedient means, expounded the Tathāgata's wisdom. We, having attained the single day's wage of nirvāṇa from the Buddha, considered it a great gain, having no aspiration to seek this Great Vehicle. Furthermore, relying on the Tathāgata's wisdom, we opened, revealed, and expounded it for Bodhisattvas, yet had no aspiration for it ourselves.
For what reason? The Buddha knew our minds delighted in small dharmas, and by the power of expedient means spoke in accord with us; yet we did not know that we were truly Buddha-children.
Now we finally know that the World-Honored One is not stingy with the Buddha's wisdom. For what reason? From the past onward, we have truly been Buddha-children, yet merely delighted in small dharmas. If we had possessed the mind to delight in the great, the Buddha would have expounded the Great Vehicle Dharma for us.
In this sūtra, he speaks solely of the One Vehicle. In the past, before the Bodhisattvas, he rebuked the Śrāvakas for delighting in small dharmas; yet the Buddha was truly teaching and transforming through the Great Vehicle.
Therefore, we say that we originally had no mind to seek anything. Now the Dharma King's great treasure has arrived spontaneously; what a Buddha-child should attain, we have completely attained!
Thereupon, Mahākāśyapa, wishing to restate this meaning, spoke gāthās:
We today,
Hearing the Buddha's voice and teaching,
Rejoice and leap,
Attaining what we never had before.
The Buddha proclaims that Śrāvakas
Will attain Buddhahood,
The unexcelled heap of treasures,
Unsought, has arrived of itself.
Just like a child,
Young and without understanding,
Who abandons his father and runs away,
Going far to another land,
Wandering through various countries
For over fifty years.
His father, grieving and longing,
Searches in all four directions;
Having grown weary of searching,
He settles in a certain city,
Building a residence,
Amusing himself with the five desires.
His household is immensely wealthy,
Possessing much gold, silver,
Mother-of-pearl, agate,
Pearls, and lapis lazuli;
Elephants, horses, oxen, sheep,
Palanquins, and carriages;
Fields, estates, servants,
And people in great numbers.
His lending and trade
Extend throughout other countries,
Merchant traders
Are everywhere.
Thousands of billions of people
Reverently surround him;
Constantly by kings
Loved and cherished,
Ministers and noble families
All venerate him.
Through various causes,
Those coming and going are many;
His wealth and power are like this,
Possessing great influence.
Yet his years are declining,
Increasing his sorrowful thoughts of his son;
Day and night he contemplates:
The time of death approaches.
My foolish son abandoned me
For over fifty years;
The goods in my storehouses,
What will become of them?
At that time, the poor son,
Seeking clothing and food,
From village to village,
From country to country,
Sometimes finding something,
Sometimes finding nothing,
Famished and emaciated,
His body breaking out in scabs and sores.
Gradually passing along,
He reaches the city where his father dwells;
Wandering as a hired laborer,
He finally arrives at his father's house.
At that time, the elder,
Within his gate,
Erected a great jewel canopy,
Seated upon a lion seat;
Surrounded by his attendants,
Guarded by people,
Some calculating
Gold, silver, and treasures,
Income and disbursement of wealth,
Recording receipts and ledgers.
The poor son, seeing his father
Noble, wealthy, and dignified,
Thought he was a king,
Or the equal of a king.
Startled, he wondered
Why he had come here.
Again he thought:
"If I remain long,
I may be oppressed
And forced into labor."
Having contemplated this,
He ran away in haste,
Inquiring after a poor village,
Wishing to seek hired work.
The elder at that time,
On the lion seat,
Saw his son from afar,
Silently recognizing him.
He immediately ordered a messenger
To pursue and bring him back.
The poor son cried out in alarm,
Fainting and falling to the ground:
"This man seizes me,
I will certainly be killed;
Why did seeking clothing and food
Bring me to this place?"
The father, knowing his son
Was foolish and base,
Would not believe his words,
Would not believe he was his father.
Immediately, through expedient means,
He dispatched other men,
One-eyed, dwarfish, ugly,
Lacking impressive demeanor:
"You speak to him,
Saying you will hire him
To clear away dung and filth,
Paying him double wages."
The poor son, hearing this,
Joyfully followed them,
Clearing away dung and filth,
Cleaning the rooms.
The elder through the window
Constantly watched his son,
Thinking his son was foolish and base,
Delighting in lowly work.
Thereupon the elder,
Wearing ragged, dirty clothes,
Holding a dung basket,
Went to where his son was,
Using expedient means to approach him,
Telling him to work diligently:
"I will increase your wages,
Give you oil for your feet,
Plentiful food and drink,
And thick, warm mats."
With such earnest words:
"You must work diligently."
And with gentle words:
"You are like my son."
The elder, possessing wisdom,
Gradually allowed him to come and go;
Passing through twenty years,
He managed the household activities.
He showed him gold, silver,
Pearls, and crystal;
The incoming and outgoing of goods,
He caused him to know them all.
Yet he remained outside the gate,
Lodging in a grass hut,
Reflecting on his poverty:
"I do not possess these things."
The father, knowing his son's mind
Had gradually broadened,
Wished to give him his wealth.
He gathered his relatives,
Kings, ministers,
Kṣatriyas, and householders.
Amidst this great assembly,
He said: "This is my son,
Who abandoned me and went elsewhere
For fifty years.
Since I saw my son,
Twenty years have passed.
In the past, in a certain city,
I lost this son;
Traveling around searching,
I finally arrived here.
All that I possess,
Houses, property, and people,
I entrust completely to him,
To use as he pleases."
The son, recalling his past poverty
And base disposition,
Now from his father
Greatly obtains rare treasures,
Along with dwellings
And all wealth.
He rejoiced exceedingly,
Attaining what he never had before.
The Buddha is also like this;
Knowing that we delighted in the small,
He never said:
"You will become Buddhas."
Instead he said that we
Attained uncontaminated dharmas,
Perfecting the Small Vehicle,
Śrāvaka disciples.
The Buddha commanded us
To speak the supreme path;
Those who cultivate this
Will attain Buddhahood.
Receiving the Buddha's teaching,
For the great Bodhisattvas,
Through various conditions,
Parables,
And many words,
We spoke the unexcelled path.
All the Buddha-children,
Hearing the Dharma from us,
Contemplated it day and night,
Diligently cultivating it.
At that time, all the Buddhas
Conferred prophecies upon them:
"You in a future age
Will attain Buddhahood."
The secret treasury of dharmas
Of all the Buddhas,
Only for Bodhisattvas
Did they expound the genuine reality,
And not for us
Did they speak this genuine essential.
Just like that poor son
Who drew near his father,
Although knowing all the goods,
His mind did not aspire to take them.
Although we spoke
Of the treasure store of the Buddha-Dharma,
We had no aspiration ourselves;
It was also like this.
We attained internal cessation,
Considering ourselves satisfied;
Understanding only this matter,
We had no other concern.
Even if we heard of
Purifying Buddha lands
And teaching wandering beings,
We had no delight in it.
For what reason?
All dharmas
Are completely empty and tranquil,
Without arising and without ceasing,
Neither great nor small,
Uncontaminated and unconditioned.
Contemplating thus,
We did not give rise to joy.
Throughout the long night,
Toward the Buddha's wisdom,
We had no greed and no attachment,
Having no further aspiration;
Regarding our own Dharma,
We considered it ultimate.
Throughout the long night,
Cultivating the Dharma of emptiness,
We escaped the three realms,
The afflictions of suffering,
Dwelling in our final bodies,
In nirvāṇa with remainder.
Taught and transformed by the Buddha,
Our attainment of the path was not false;
We considered that we had already
Repaid the Buddha's kindness.
Although for
All the Buddha-children,
We expounded the Bodhisattva Dharma
To seek the Buddha path,
Toward this Dharma,
We never had aspiration or joy.
The Guide, seeing our minds,
Left us alone;
At first he did not encourage us,
Speaking of genuine benefit.
Just as the rich elder,
Knowing his son's base disposition,
Through the power of expedient means,
Subdued his mind,
And only afterward entrusted
All his wealth to him:
The Buddha is also like this,
Displaying rare matters;
Knowing those who delight in the small,
Through the power of expedient means,
He subdues their minds,
And only then teaches the great wisdom.
We today
Have attained what we never had before;
What was not originally expected
Has now arrived of itself,
Just like that poor son
Attaining immeasurable treasures.
World-Honored One, we now
Have attained the path and its fruit,
Within uncontaminated dharmas
Attaining the pure eye.
Throughout the long night,
Upholding the Buddha's pure precepts,
Only beginning today
Do we attain their fruit and retribution;
Within the Dharma of the Dharma King,
Long cultivating Brahma conduct,
We now attain the uncontaminated,
Unexcelled great fruit.
We now
Are genuine Śrāvakas,
Using the sound of the Buddha path
To cause all to hear.
We now
Are genuine Arhats;
In all the worlds,
Amidst devas, humans, Maras, and Brahmās,
Universally in their midst,
We are worthy of receiving offerings.
The World-Honored One's great kindness,
Through rare matters,
Pitying, teaching, and transforming,
Benefits us;
Throughout immeasurable billions of kalpas,
Who could repay it?
Serving with hands and feet,
Bowing with the crown of the head,
All kinds of offerings,
None can repay it.
Even if bearing him on the crown,
Carrying him on both shoulders,
Throughout kalpas like Ganges sands,
Exhausting one's heart in reverence;
Or with delicious food,
Immeasurable precious robes,
Bedding,
Various medicinal broths,
Ox-head sandalwood,
And various treasures,
Erecting stūpas and shrines,
Spreading precious robes on the ground—
Using such things as these
To make offerings
Throughout kalpas like Ganges sands,
Still one could not repay it.
The Buddhas are rare,
Immeasurable, boundless,
Inconceivable,
Endowed with great spiritual powers,
Uncontaminated and unconditioned,
Kings of all dharmas.
Able, for the sake of the base,
To endure these matters,
To common mortals who grasp marks,
Expounding according to what is appropriate.
The Buddhas, regarding dharmas,
Attain supreme mastery;
Knowing the various desires,
Joys, and mental capacities
Of all wandering beings,
According to what they can bear,
Through immeasurable parables,
They expound the Dharma for them.
Accordant with wandering beings'
Wholesome roots of past lives,
Knowing who have ripened
And who have not yet ripened,
Pondering variously,
Distinguishing and knowing them,
Within the One Vehicle path,
They expound three according to what is appropriate.
