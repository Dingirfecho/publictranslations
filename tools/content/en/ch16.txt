At that time, the Buddha addressed the Bodhisattvas and the entire great assembly: "Good children! You should believe and understand the Tathāgata's genuine and sincere words."
He addressed the great assembly again: "You should believe and understand the Tathāgata's genuine and sincere words."
And yet again he addressed the great assembly: "You should believe and understand the Tathāgata's genuine and sincere words."
At that time, the great assembly of Bodhisattvas, with Maitreya at their head, joined their palms and addressed the Buddha: "World-Honored One! We only pray that you will speak it; we will believe and accept the Buddha's words."
Having spoken thus three times, they said again: "We only pray that you will speak it; we will believe and accept the Buddha's words."
Then the World-Honored One, knowing that the Bodhisattvas did not cease after three requests, addressed them: "Listen attentively to the Tathāgata's secret and spiritual powers!
All devas, humans, and asuras in the world consider that the present Śākyamuni Buddha, having left the Śākya palace, went not far from the city of Gayā, sat at the place of awakening, and attained anuttarasamyaksambodhi.
However, Good children! It has been immeasurable, boundless hundreds of thousands of millions of inconceivable numbers of kalpas since I truly attained Buddhahood.
Suppose there were five hundred million billion inconceivable incalculable great three-thousand-fold worlds; if someone were to pulverize them into dust motes, and passing eastward across five hundred million billion inconceivable incalculable lands, dropped a single dust mote; continuing eastward in this manner until all these dust motes were exhausted. Good children! What do you think: could these worlds be conceived, calculated, and their numbers known?"
Maitreya Bodhisattva and the others addressed the Buddha together: "World-Honored One! These worlds are immeasurable, boundless, beyond the reach of calculation, nor can mental power reach them. All Śrāvakas and Pratyekabuddhas, with their uncontaminated wisdom, cannot conceive or know their limits. We, dwelling on the ground of not-returning, also cannot penetrate this matter. World-Honored One! Such worlds as these are immeasurable and boundless."
Then the Buddha addressed the assembly of great Bodhisattvas: "Good children! I will now clearly proclaim to you: if all these worlds, both where dust motes were dropped and where none were dropped, were entirely pulverized into dust motes, each dust mote counted as a kalpa, the time since I attained Buddhahood exceeds even this by hundreds of thousands of millions of inconceivable numbers of incalculable kalpas.
From that time onward, I have constantly been in this Sahā world expounding the Dharma, teaching and transforming; and in other places, in hundreds of thousands of millions of inconceivable numbers of incalculable lands, guiding and benefiting wandering beings.
Good children! During this interval, I spoke of Dīpaṃkara Buddha and others, and also spoke of them entering nirvāṇa; all these were distinguished through expedient means.
Good children! If there are wandering beings who come to where I am, I, with the Buddha eye, observe whether their faculties of faith and the like are sharp or dull, and according to what is appropriate to liberate them, in various places I speak of myself under different names and differing ages, and also manifest saying that I will enter nirvāṇa; through various expedient means, I expound the subtle, wondrous Dharma, able to cause wandering beings to generate joyful minds.
Good children! The Tathāgata, seeing wandering beings who delight in small dharmas, of meager virtue and heavy defilements, speaks to these persons: 'In my youth I left home and attained anuttarasamyaksambodhi.' Yet in truth, since I attained Buddhahood it has been long and distant like this; it is merely through expedient means to teach and transform wandering beings, causing them to enter the Buddha path, that I speak thus.
Good children! The scriptures expounded by the Tathāgata are all for the sake of liberating wandering beings: whether speaking of his own body or speaking of other bodies; whether displaying his own body or displaying other bodies; whether displaying his own deeds or displaying other deeds—all that is spoken is genuine and without falsehood.
For what reason? The Tathāgata knows and sees the marks of the three realms as they truly are: without birth and death, without receding or emerging; neither dwelling in the world nor entering extinction; non-real, non-empty, non-such, non-different; not viewing the three realms as the three realms view them. Matters such as these, the Tathāgata sees clearly without error.
Because wandering beings have various natures, various desires, various actions, and various recollections and discriminations, wishing to cause them to give rise to wholesome roots, through various causal conditions, parables, and words, he expounds the Dharma in various ways; the Buddha activities performed have never ceased for an instant.
Thus, since I attained Buddhahood, it has been exceedingly long and distant; my lifespan is immeasurable, incalculable kalpas, constantly abiding and never extinguished.
Good children! The lifespan I accomplished by originally practicing the Bodhisattva path is even now not yet exhausted, and exceeds the previous number twofold. Yet now, not being genuine extinction, I proclaim: 'I will take extinction.' Through this expedient means, the Tathāgata teaches and transforms wandering beings.
For what reason? If the Buddha were to abide long in the world, persons of meager virtue would not plant wholesome roots; poor and base, greedily attached to the five desires, they would enter the net of recollections and false views. If they saw that the Tathāgata was constantly present and not extinguished, they would give rise to arrogance and become negligent, unable to generate the thought that he is difficult to encounter, or a mind of reverence.
Therefore, the Tathāgata expediently speaks: 'Bhikṣus, you should know! The appearance of Buddhas in the world is difficult to encounter.'
For what reason? Persons of meager virtue, passing through immeasurable hundreds of thousands of millions of kalpas, some behold a Buddha, and some do not; on account of this matter, I speak these words: 'Bhikṣus! The Tathāgata is difficult to behold.' These wandering beings, hearing such words, will certainly give rise to the thought that he is difficult to encounter, harboring longing in their hearts, thirsting for the Buddha, and will then plant wholesome roots. Therefore, although the Tathāgata does not truly pass into extinction, he speaks of extinction.
Furthermore, Good children! The Dharma of all Buddhas, the Tathāgatas, is like this; for the sake of liberating wandering beings, all is genuine and without falsehood.
Suppose a skilled physician, intelligent and clear-sighted, versed in formulas and medicines, skillfully treats all diseases. The man has many children—ten, twenty, or up to a hundred. Due to some affair, he travels far to another country.
Afterward, the children drink poisonous medicine from elsewhere; the medicine takes effect, causing delirium and agony, and they roll writhing upon the ground.
At that time, their father returns home. Among the children who drank the poison, some have lost their original senses, while others have not. Seeing their father from afar, they all rejoice greatly, kneeling and inquiring: 'Peacefully and safely have you returned! We, foolish and deluded, mistakenly took poisonous medicine; pray rescue and treat us, and grant us life again!'
The father, seeing his children afflicted with such suffering, relies on formulas and seeks fine medicinal herbs, fully endowed with color, scent, and fine flavor, poundings, siftings, and mixing them together, giving them to his children to take, saying: 'This great, excellent medicine is fully endowed with color, scent, and fine flavor; you may take it; it will quickly eliminate suffering and afflictions, and you will have no further tribulations.'
Those among the children who have not lost their senses, seeing this excellent medicine with both color and scent being fine, immediately take it, and their illness is completely cured.
The others who have lost their senses, seeing their father arrive, although also joyfully inquiring and seeking treatment for their illness, when given the medicine, refuse to take it.
For what reason? Because the poison has penetrated deeply, causing them to lose their original senses; regarding this medicine of fine color and scent, they consider it not delicious.
The father has this thought: 'These children are to be pitied; struck by poison, their minds are entirely inverted. Although seeing me with joy and seeking treatment, they refuse to take such fine medicine. I must now devise an expedient means to cause them to take this medicine.'
Immediately he says: 'You should know! I am now old and decrepit, and the time of death has arrived. This fine medicine I now leave here; you may take it; do not worry that you will not recover.'
Having given this instruction, he travels again to another country, and dispatches a messenger to return and announce: 'Your father has already died.'
At that time, the children, hearing that their father has passed away, are grieved and afflicted in heart, thinking: 'If our father were alive, he would pity us and rescue us; now he has abandoned us and died far away in another country.' Reflecting that they are orphaned and destitute, having no further protector, constantly harboring sorrow, their minds finally awaken; they then realize that this medicine has fine color, scent, and flavor, immediately take it, and the illness of poison is completely cured. The father, hearing that his children have all recovered, soon returns, causing them all to behold him.
Good children! What do you think: could anyone say that this skilled physician is guilty of falsehood?"
"No, World-Honored One!"
The Buddha said: "I too am like this. Since I attained Buddhahood, it has been immeasurable, boundless hundreds of thousands of millions of inconceivable numbers of incalculable kalpas; for the sake of wandering beings, through the power of expedient means, I say that I will take extinction; yet none can say according to Dharma that I am guilty of falsehood."
Thereupon, the World-Honored One, wishing to restate this meaning, spoke gāthās:
Since I attained Buddhahood,
The kalpas that have elapsed,
Are immeasurable hundreds of thousands,
Tens of billions of ages incalculable;
Constantly expounding the Dharma, teaching and transforming
Countless billions of wandering beings,
Causing them to enter the Buddha path.
Since then, throughout immeasurable kalpas,
For the sake of liberating wandering beings,
Expediently I manifest nirvāṇa;
Yet in truth I do not pass into extinction,
Constantly dwelling here to expound the Dharma.
I constantly abide here,
Using all spiritual powers,
So that perverted wandering beings,
Though near, do not see me.
Multitudes seeing me enter extinction,
Widely make offerings to my relics,
All harboring longing in their hearts,
Giving rise to thirsty yearning.
When wandering beings have become faithful and subdued,
Upright and gentle in mind,
Single-mindedly wishing to behold the Buddha,
Not begrudging physical life;
Then I, together with the Saṅgha,
Appear on Vulture Peak.
I then tell wandering beings:
"I am constantly here without extinction;
Through the power of expedient means,
I manifest having extinction and non-extinction."
In other lands where there are wandering beings,
Reverent, faithful, and joyful,
I also in their midst,
Expound the unexcelled Dharma.
You, not hearing this,
Merely consider that I have passed into extinction.
I see wandering beings,
Submerged in suffering and afflictions;
Therefore I do not manifest my body,
Causing them to give rise to thirsty yearning;
Because of their hearts' longing,
I then appear to expound the Dharma for them.
Spiritual powers are like this,
Throughout incalculable kalpas,
Constantly on Vulture Peak,
And in various other dwellings.
When wandering beings see the kalpa end,
Burned by a great fire,
This land of mine is safe and peaceful,
Devas and humans constantly filling it.
Gardens, groves, halls, and pavilions,
Adorned with various treasures,
Jewel trees with abundant flowers and fruits,
Where wandering beings play and rejoice.
Devas strike heavenly drums,
Constantly making various music,
Raining down māndārava flowers,
Scattering them over the Buddha and the great assembly.
My pure land is undestroyed,
Yet the multitudes see it burned away,
Grief, terror, and all suffering,
Completely filling it like this.
These sinful wandering beings,
Through causal conditions of evil karma,
Pass through incalculable kalpas,
Without hearing the names of the Three Jewels.
Those who cultivate merits,
Gentle, upright, and direct,
All behold my body,
Dwelling here and expounding the Dharma.
At times for this assembly,
Speaking of the Buddha's immeasurable lifespan;
For those who only after long behold the Buddha,
Speaking of the Buddha being difficult to meet.
My power of wisdom is like this,
The light of wisdom illuminating boundlessly,
Lifespan of countless kalpas,
Attained through long cultivation of karma.
You who possess wisdom,
Do not give rise to doubt in this!
Sever it and cause it to end forever;
The Buddha's words are real and not false.
Like the physician skillfully using expedient means,
To cure his deranged children,
Being truly alive, yet saying he has died;
None can say he spoke falsehood.
I too am the father of the world,
Rescuing all from suffering and affliction;
Because common mortals are perverted,
Being truly present, I say I pass into extinction.
Because constantly seeing me,
They give rise to arrogant minds,
Negligent, attached to the five desires,
Falling into evil destinies.
I constantly know wandering beings,
Whether practicing the path or not practicing the path;
According to what is appropriate to liberate them,
I expound various dharmas for them.
Constantly having this thought within myself:
"By what means can I cause wandering beings,
To enter unexcelled wisdom,
And swiftly accomplish the Buddha-body?"
